Under the Wave off Kanagawa

| Katsushika Hokusai
iCatalog details

Japanese, 1760–1849 · ca. 1830–32 · Woodblock print; ink and color on paper, 25.7 × 37.9 cm
The Metropolitan Museum of Art, JP1847 · Public domain (CC0) · metmuseum.org

From Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji, and the reason most people outside Japan can picture a Hokusai at all. Fuji is in it, small, sitting under the break of the wave — the mountain is the whole point of the series and here it is nearly upstaged. The blue is Prussian blue, imported and by the 1830s cheap enough to print in quantity, which is a good part of why the series looks the way it does.

Snowscape with Figures

| Kim Si
iCatalog details

Korean, 1524–1593 · 1584 · Hanging scroll; ink on silk, 53 × 67.3 cm (painting only)
The Cleveland Museum of Art, 1987.187 · Public domain (CC0) · clevelandart.org

Dated by its own inscription to 1584 and signed by Kim Si, a Joseon painter who worked in the manner of the Chinese Zhe school and was known for winter scenes. The snow is not painted; it is the untouched silk, with the ink laid in around it, so the brightest thing in the picture is the part the brush never reached. The silk has darkened over four centuries, which has narrowed the contrast the painter was working with.

Plum Garden at Kamata

| Utagawa Hiroshige
iCatalog details

Japanese, 1797–1858 · 1857 · Woodblock print (nishiki-e); ink and color on paper, 33.7 × 21.9 cm
The Metropolitan Museum of Art, JP2510 · Public domain (CC0) · metmuseum.org

One of the hundred views Hiroshige made of Edo and its outskirts in the last years of his life. Kamata, south of the city, was somewhere people traveled to specifically to see the plums in flower, and the print is built around that: trunks in the near ground, blossom across the middle, and the rest given over to a flat sky laid in as a graded wash. The red is as much the subject as the trees are.

Black-naped Oriole Perched on a Stem of Rose Mallow

| Utagawa Hiroshige
iCatalog details

Japanese, 1797–1858 · ca. 1834 · Woodblock print; ink and color on paper, 37.3 × 12.5 cm
The Metropolitan Museum of Art, JP256 · Public domain (CC0) · metmuseum.org

A bird-and-flower print in the tall narrow format cut to hang on a pillar, which is why it is barely a hand across and more than a foot down. The shape does the work: the stem enters at the top, the bird hangs off it at the middle, the flower opens at the foot, and the eye has to travel the full drop of the sheet. Hiroshige made these by the hundred alongside the landscapes he is better known for.

Streams and Mountains

| Xu Ben
iCatalog details

Chinese, 1335–1380 · 1372 · Hanging scroll; ink on paper, image 92.2 × 37.6 cm
The Cleveland Museum of Art, 1997.96 · Public domain (CC0) · clevelandart.org

Painted during one of the stretches Xu Ben spent in retreat from official life, in the years when the Yuan dynasty was giving way to the Ming. The mountain is built almost entirely from repeated hemp-fiber strokes, so the rock reads as one continuous rhythm rather than as separate masses stacked up. At the base, a scholar comes up through the trees on foot toward the hermit's house.

Nur Jahan holding a portrait of Emperor Jahangir

| Bishandas
iCatalog details

Indian, active early 1600s · c. 1627 · Gum tempera and gold on paper; painting 13.6 × 6.4 cm, page 30 × 22.1 cm
The Cleveland Museum of Art, 2013.325 · Public domain (CC0) · clevelandart.org

Nur Jahan was the emperor Jahangir's favorite wife and ran much of the business of the state in his name. She holds his portrait here after his death, shown in an idealized youth — she was about fifty when he died — while he appears aged behind the rail that marked imperial presence. The painting itself is only about five inches tall; everything outside that narrow panel is the album border.

Hen and Rooster with Grapevine

| Itō Jakuchū
iCatalog details

Japanese, 1716–1800 · 1792, Edo period · Hanging scroll; ink and color on silk, image 101.9 × 41.3 cm
The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 1975.268.69 · Public domain · metmuseum.org

Jakuchū ran his family's Kyoto greengrocery for forty years before handing it over to paint full time, and he was seventy-six when he made this. The bird is assembled from hundreds of separate feather strokes rather than massed shapes, and its tail is drawn crossing in front of the grapevine instead of disappearing behind it.

Ghosts of the Taira at Daimotsu Bay

| Utagawa Kuniyoshi
iCatalog details

Japanese, 1797–1861 · 1843–47 · Triptych of woodblock prints (nishiki-e); ink and color on paper
The Metropolitan Museum of Art, JP1565 · Public domain · metmuseum.org

Yoshitsune's ship driven into Daimotsu Bay by a storm and set upon by the ghosts of the Taira warriors drowned at Dan-no-ura — the clan his own campaign destroyed. Kuniyoshi returned to the subject more than once. It is a triptych: three separate sheets, designed to hang together so the wave runs unbroken across all three.
