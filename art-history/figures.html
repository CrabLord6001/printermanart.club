The Siesta

| Paul Gauguin
iCatalog details

French, Paris 1848–1903 Atuona, Hiva Oa, Marquesas Islands · ca. 1892–94 · Oil on canvas, 88.9 x 116.2 cm
The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 1993.400.3 · Public domain (CC0) · metmuseum.org

Tahitian women resting on a veranda in the long European dresses the missions brought with them, which is some of what the picture is about. Gauguin began it during his first stay on the island and was still working on it after he got back to Paris. The planking runs hard across the foreground in a different key from everything above it, so the floor almost reads as a separate painting laid underneath the figures.

Pygmalion and Galatea

| Jean-Léon Gérôme
iCatalog details

French, Vesoul 1824–1904 Paris · ca. 1890 · Oil on canvas, 88.9 × 68.6 cm
The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 27.200 · Public domain (CC0) · metmuseum.org

Gérôme painted this subject several times; the Met's version shows the figure from behind, with the change from stone to flesh caught partway up her legs. He was a sculptor as well as a painter and made a marble of the same pair, so the studio around them — the turntable, the wooden steps, the mallet on the floor — is his own. The masks hanging on the back wall are a reminder that the whole thing is staged.

La Berceuse

| Vincent van Gogh
iCatalog details

Dutch, Zundert 1853–1890 Auvers-sur-Oise · 1889 · Oil on canvas, 92.7 × 73.7 cm
The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 1996.435 · Public domain (CC0) · metmuseum.org

Augustine Roulin, the postman's wife, holding the rope that rocked a cradle just outside the picture. Van Gogh painted five versions in Arles and meant them to hang between two sunflower canvases like a shrine — something, he said, a sailor far from home might want to look at. He began the first of them in the weeks around the breakdown that cost him his ear. The wallpaper behind her is doing as much as she is: a flat green-blue field crowded with flowers that refuses to recede.

Julie Le Brun (1780–1819) Looking in a Mirror

| Elisabeth Louise Vigée Le Brun
iCatalog details

French, Paris 1755–1842 Paris · 1787 · Oil on canvas, 73 × 59.4 cm
The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 2019.141.23 · Public domain (CC0) · metmuseum.org

The painter's daughter, about seven, holding a mirror. Vigée Le Brun gives her two faces at once — the real child in profile, the reflection turned out to meet the viewer — so the picture is both a portrait and a small argument about what a portrait is. It was painted in 1787, two years before the Revolution sent her out of France; she spent twelve years working in Italy, Austria and Russia before she came back.

Faustine Léo (1832–1865)

| Henri Lehmann
iCatalog details

French, Kiel 1814–1882 Paris · 1842 · Oil on canvas, 100 × 81.3 cm
The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 2004.243 · Public domain (CC0) · metmuseum.org

Lehmann trained under Ingres, and this is Ingres's manner at one remove: the drawing kept hard, the surface polished until the brush disappears entirely. Faustine Léo was about ten when she sat for it and died at thirty-three. The cold blue ground is doing most of the work, pushing the pink dress forward and leaving the little landscape at her elbow looking like something glimpsed through a window.

Young Woman Knitting

| Berthe Morisot
iCatalog details

French, 1841–1895 · ca. 1883 · Oil on canvas, 50.2 × 60 cm
The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 67.187.89 · Public domain · metmuseum.org

Morisot was a founding member of the Impressionist group and showed in all but one of its eight exhibitions. The paint is left open here — the gravel path and the empty chair are barely more than dragged strokes over the ground — and the hands, the one part of the picture actually doing work, are the least finished thing in it. Her signature sits at the lower left in the same thin paint as the garden.

The Birth of Venus

| Alexandre Cabanel
iCatalog details

French, 1823–1889 · 1875 · Oil on canvas, 106 × 182.6 cm
The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 94.24.1 · Public domain · metmuseum.org

This is the Metropolitan's picture, painted in 1875 for the New York collector John Wolfe; the first and slightly larger version made a sensation at the Salon of 1863, was bought by Napoleon III, and is now in the Musée d'Orsay. Cabanel finishes the sea as smoothly as the figure and leaves no visible brushwork anywhere, which is precisely what the next generation of French painters would attack. Venus does not stand on a shell here but lies along the wave, arm across her face, somewhere between waking and not.

Madame X (Virginie Amélie Avegno Gautreau)

| John Singer Sargent
iCatalog details

American, 1856–1925 · 1883–84 · Oil on canvas, 208.6 × 109.9 cm
The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 16.53 · Public domain · metmuseum.org

The 1884 Paris Salon received it so badly that Sargent left for London and stayed. Gautreau powdered her skin with lavender, and Sargent matched it exactly, which is why she reads almost gray against the black dress and brown ground. The right strap originally hung off her shoulder; he repainted it upright after the Salon, and the picture stayed in his studio for thirty years.
