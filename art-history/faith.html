The Penitent Magdalen

| Georges de La Tour
iCatalogue details

French, Vic-sur-Seille 1593–1652 Lunéville · ca. 1640 · Oil on canvas, 133.4 × 102.2 cm
The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 1978.517 · Public domain (CC0) · metmuseum.org

One of several Magdalens La Tour painted; this is the Met's, sometimes called the Magdalen with Two Flames for the candle and its reflection. The jewelry is pushed to the edge of the table and the skull sits in her lap instead, which is the whole argument of the picture made without a word. La Tour was forgotten for most of three centuries and only rebuilt into the canon after 1915.

The Rest on the Flight into Egypt

| Gerard David
iCatalogue details

Netherlandish, ca. 1455–1523 · ca. 1512–15 · Oil on wood, 53.3 × 39.8 cm
The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 49.7.21 · Public domain (CC0) · metmuseum.org

The subject is a rest stop on the journey out of Judea, and David sets it in a Netherlandish wood — oaks, a worn path, a town in the distance — rather than anywhere near Egypt. The Virgin takes the whole center of the panel, in a blue that would have been the most expensive material in it. Small figures keep the journey going in the background, so the picture holds the pause and the road at the same time.

Icon of the Virgin and Child, Hodegetria variant

| Byzantine or Crusader
iCatalogue details

13th century · 13th century · Tempera on wood, 25.4 × 18.4 × 1.3 cm
The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 2020.401 · Public domain · metmuseum.org

Hodegetria means she who shows the way, and names the gesture: the Virgin’s free hand points to the child rather than to herself. The type was copied for centuries from a lost icon in Constantinople said to have been painted from life. This panel is small enough to hold, and was made in the eastern Mediterranean during the Crusader period, when Byzantine and Western workshops were working side by side and not always separably.

The Crucifixion

| Fra Angelico
iCatalogue details

Italian, ca. 1395–1455 · ca. 1420–23 · Tempera on wood, gold ground, 63.8 × 48.3 cm
The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 43.98.5 · Public domain · metmuseum.org

An early panel, small enough that the Metropolitan takes it to have been made for private devotion rather than for an altar. Mourners, soldiers and mounted riders close in a ring around the foot of the cross, a circular arrangement the museum traces to Lorenzo Ghiberti's bronze doors for the Florence Baptistery. Behind the cross there is no sky at all, only gold.

Icon of the Mother of God and Infant Christ (Virgin Eleousa)

| Attributed to Angelos Akotantos
iCatalogue details

Cretan, died 1450 · c. 1425–50 · Tempera and gold on wood, 96 × 70 cm
The Cleveland Museum of Art, 2010.154 · Public domain (CC0) · clevelandart.org

Painted on Crete while the island was under Venetian rule, and large enough to have stood on an iconostasis, the screen that closes off the sanctuary in a Greek Orthodox church. The Eleousa, or tenderness, type puts mother and child cheek to cheek rather than facing the congregation straight on. Cleveland gives the panel to Angelos Akotantos, the most sought-after painter in Candia, as an attribution rather than a signature.

The Crucifixion of Saint Andrew

| Caravaggio
iCatalogue details

Italian, 1571–1610 · 1606–7 · Oil on canvas, 202.5 × 152.7 cm
The Cleveland Museum of Art, 1976.2 · Public domain (CC0) · clevelandart.org

Painted in Naples, in the months after Caravaggio fled Rome under sentence of death for killing a man. The legend holds that Andrew preached from the cross for two days until the crowd demanded his release, and that the executioner sent to cut him down found his hands would not move. That is the instant here: the man on the ladder, arms up, stopped.
