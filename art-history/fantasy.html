The Titan's Goblet

| Thomas Cole
iCatalogue details

American, Lancashire 1801–1848 Catskill, New York · 1833 · Oil on canvas, 49.2 × 41 cm
The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 04.29.2 · Public domain (CC0) · metmuseum.org

Cole never explained it and nobody since has settled it. The rim of the goblet holds a sea of its own, with ships on it, a Greek temple on one shore and a palace on the other, while below the stem an ordinary landscape carries on as though nothing were standing in it. Readings have reached for Norse myth, for Greek myth, and for the painter's own dreams. It is also a small painting — nineteen inches by sixteen — which makes the scale inside it stranger than a large one would.

Return of the King of the Dead

| Michael Whelan
iCatalogue details

American, born 1950 · 2025 · Acrylic on Gessobord, 12 × 9 in.
Artist's own collection · In copyright · michaelwhelan.com

Whelan calls this a sequel to his own cover for Michael Moorcock's The Bane of the Black Sword, painted decades later and on his own account rather than to a commission. It is small — twelve inches by nine — and in acrylic on panel, which is close quarters for a picture holding an armored giant and a swordsman. He has said it was mostly done as a goof, while adding that the symbolism in it is deliberate.

Pandora

| Odilon Redon
iCatalogue details

French, 1840–1916 · ca. 1914 · Oil on canvas, 143.5 × 62.2 cm
The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 60.19.1 · Public domain (CC0) · metmuseum.org

Redon spent the first half of his career working almost entirely in black, in charcoal and lithography, and the second half in color; this belongs to the end of the second. Pandora holds the box, but the myth is barely the point — the flowers get more attention than she does, scattered in the loose patches of color he used in his flower paintings. He was in his seventies when he painted it.

Jewels from the Deep

| Arthur Rackham
iCatalogue details

English, London 1867–1939 Limpsfield · 1909 · Pen and ink with watercolor
Private collection · Public domain

Catalogued only as Jewels from the Deep, 1909 — which is the year Rackham's Undine appeared and the obvious guess, except that the first edition's own list of fifteen plates does not include it. Historians still are uncertain of the book it belonged to, if it did at all. The method is the usual Rackham: a pen line laid down first and watercolor washed over it in thin coats, which is why the figures look drawn and the water looks stained.

The Fairy Tale Prince

| John Bauer
iCatalogue details

Swedish, Jönköping 1882–1918 Lake Vättern · ca. 1907 · Watercolor and ink on paper
Private collection · Public domain

The signature in the corner is certain. Almost nothing else is. Print sellers list it as The Fairy Tale Prince — Sagoprinsen — but Bukowskis attaches that same title to a different Bauer altogether, a young nobleman with a white horse and no snow in sight, while other listings give only “from a Christmas book, ca. 1907” and decline to name which book. Bauer drew for the Swedish Christmas annuals every year in that stretch, so an annual is the likely answer and may well stay only likely. The content of the image here is simpler than the history surrounding it: a crown, a long cloak, deep snow, and a troll barely waist-high who is entirely unimpressed.

Cover of Amazing Stories, July 1927

| Frank R. Paul
iCatalogue details

American, born Austria, 1884–1963 · 1927 · Color process printing on pulp paper
Wikimedia Commons · Public domain · commons.wikimedia.org

Paul drew nearly every cover of Amazing Stories in its first years, and largely invented how science fiction looked before anyone had agreed what it was. He was trained as an architect and draftsman, which shows in the machinery: the hardware is drawn as though it could be built, and the people are there mostly to give it scale. This issue carried the second installment of A. Merritt’s The Moon Pool.

The Books of Earthsea: The Complete Illustrated Edition

| Charles Vess
iCatalogue details

American, born 1951 · 2018 · More than fifty color and black-and-white illustrations
Saga Press, New York · In copyright · greenmanpress.com

Four years of work, made in close collaboration with Ursula K. Le Guin, who chose the scenes herself and died months before the book appeared. The dragon alone took about a year of revision, each sketch pulling it further from a reptile and closer to a face. Vess keeps the figures small inside large landscapes, so it is the islands rather than the wizards that carry the pictures.

Oedipus and the Sphinx

| Gustave Moreau
iCatalogue details

French, 1826–1898 · 1864 · Oil on canvas, 206.4 × 104.8 cm
The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 21.134.1 · Public domain · metmuseum.org

Moreau made his name with this picture at the Paris Salon of 1864, at a moment when French painting was turning toward scenes of ordinary modern life. He went the other way, into myth, and a younger generation that included Odilon Redon and Oscar Wilde followed him there. The sphinx has jumped and is holding on by her claws: the riddle is being put at close range, and what is left of the travelers who answered wrong lies on the ledge below.

The Beacons of Gondor

| Donato Giancola
iCatalogue details

American, born 1967 · 2022 · Oil on linen, 77 × 114 in.
Artist's own collection · In copyright · donatoarts.com

Nearly ten feet across, painted in traditional oils from posed models rather than digitally. The beacon fire is the only warm light in the picture, so the whole composition is built out of how far that light carries before it gives out.

Island of the Dead

| Arnold Böcklin
iCatalogue details

Swiss, 1827–1901 · 1880 · Oil on wood, 73.7 × 121.9 cm
The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 26.90 · Public domain · metmuseum.org

The island exists nowhere. A young widow commissioned the picture as a memorial to her husband, and the draped coffin and the white standing figure were added at her request. Cliffs and cypresses drop to near-silhouette, so the only truly lit thing in the painting is the small boat. This is the first of five versions Böcklin painted; the one usually reproduced online is the 1883 Berlin picture.
